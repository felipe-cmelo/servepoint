Some court documents have to be personally served. This guide describes what the Supreme Court says about personal service and about proving it, and points to the rules and forms so you can read them in full. It does not say which method of service your matter requires. Your firm decides that.

## What personal service means

The Supreme Court's own guidance describes personal service as physically leaving the documents with the person you are serving. The documents that must be served this way, such as a Notice of Civil Claim and a petition, are listed in Rule 4-3(1) of the Supreme Court Civil Rules. Read that rule for the list and for how it applies to individuals and to companies.

## Who can serve

The court's guidance says you can serve documents yourself or have someone else do it for you. A professional process server is one option, particularly when you expect difficulty reaching the person.

## How service is proved

The court's guidance says it is important to be able to prove that the other party was served, because otherwise they could say they did not know about the claim.

- For personal service, proof is an affidavit of personal service in Form 15, under Rule 4-6.
- If the other party files a response, the court's guidance says that shows they were served.
- Ordinary service has its own forms. The List of Forms in the Civil Rules shows Form 16 (affidavit of ordinary service) and the rules that cite them.

Check the current text of Rule 4-6 and the forms before you rely on any of this.

## What a record of attempts looks like

A person serving documents swears an affidavit about what happened.
